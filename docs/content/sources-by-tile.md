# Open sources by tile

This document lists the open reference sources found for each of the twelve encyclopedia
tiles, and states how each tile's content shifts when it is written as a general civilian
guide instead of an edited chapter of FM 21-76. It is the input to the per-tile approval
process (information audit, copy review, RAG tests). Nothing here is approved yet.

## Conventions

- One section per tile, in the PRD 1.3 priority order. Each section has the same five parts:
  Current narrative, Sources, Datasets (where any apply), Gaps, Narrative shift.
- "New vs ledger" compares against the sources flux already pulled (`flux/box/data/universal.tsv`,
  69 rows) and the notes in `flux/docs/research/`. "Yes" means the source is absent from both.
- Every listed source was opened on 2026-08-24 and its licence statement read. "Unknown" in
  the Licence column means the page carries no statement; "link only" in Gaps means the source
  is authoritative but its terms block carrying it in the pack (all rights reserved,
  NoDerivatives, sign-up gate).
- Several hosts refused automated fetches from the research environment on 2026-08-24:
  cdc.gov and stacks.cdc.gov, fema.gov, nrc.gov, phmsa.dot.gov, journals.sagepub.com (Wilderness
  Medical Society), ahajournals.org. Rows that cite those hosts were read through NCBI Bookshelf,
  the Internet Archive, or a mirror, and each such row says so. Re-verify those rows from a
  browser before the audit.
- Licence posture: the project is a non-profit research artifact. Public domain, CC0, CC BY,
  CC BY-SA, CC BY-NC, CC BY-NC-SA and open government licences are usable. NoDerivatives terms and
  access gates block.

Three findings hold across tiles:

1. The ledger's centre of gravity moves from 1900s public-domain books and the 1992 manual
   to current agency guidance: CDC Yellow Book 2026 chapters on NCBI Bookshelf (public
   domain), USDA FSIS, USGS, NWS, NPS, USFS, Ready.gov, EPA. Every tile's narrative-shift
   list names one of these as the new primary authority.
2. The professional-society guidelines a clinician would cite first (Wilderness Medical
   Society, AHA and Red Cross 2024, ANZCOR) are copyright and stay link only in every tile
   that needs them. The open substitutes are IFRC 2020, the CDC chapters, and the Army
   TB MED and ATP series with public-release statements.
3. Eleven of twelve reports move FM 21-76's own chapter to an archived or appendix layer,
   quoted where the new authority is silent (improvised stills, debris hut, Dakota hole,
   shadow-tip, snare and deadfall walks, shielding physics). The manual stays a source; it
   stops being the frame.

## Tile 1: Survival Medicine

### Current narrative

FM 21-76 chapter 4 runs from hygiene and water discipline through the lifesaving steps, bleeding control, fractures and dislocations, bites and stings, wound care, heat and cold injuries, and a closing section on herbal remedies. It addresses a soldier alone or in a buddy pair after an aircraft or vehicle loss, and it assumes no evacuation for days. Its protocols date from 1992: it tells a buddy to loosen a tourniquet every 10 to 15 minutes, subordinates the tourniquet to every other method, gives a pressure-point figure, and tells the reader to smother an attached tick with Vaseline or tree sap. It carries seven figures for the whole chapter. A civilian who reads for half an hour a day gets no CPR sequence, no AED, no anaphylaxis, no choking, no burns, no medication guidance, and no rule for when to call for help, which is the first decision a civilian makes.

### Narrative shift

- Make IFRC 2020 the tile's primary authority for lay first aid, with ATP 4-02.11 (March 2026) as the primary authority for bleeding control, splinting, burns, bites, and heat and cold injury text, and FM 4-25.11 as the figure source. FM 21-76 chapter 4 keeps one contribution, the improvised traction splint, and moves to an archive note.
- Open the tile with the civilian decision the FM never states: assess the scene, check response and breathing, call for help or send for it, then act. IFRC's "Assess the scene", "Assess the person" and "Access help" skill cards (pages 402 to 405) and the WHO BEC ABCDE approach supply this order.
- Add the topics the FM omits and the civilian reader meets most: CPR and AED, choking, anaphylaxis, stroke and chest pain, seizure, burns, blisters, sprains, and dehydration. Every one has an IFRC 2020 section with graded recommendations.
- Replace the FM bleeding sequence with the current one: direct pressure, then wound packing, then tourniquet placed early for life-threatening limb bleeding, with the TCCC 2024 rules of conversion under 2 hours and no removal after 6 hours in the field. Publish the FM's loosen-every-15-minutes instruction only as a flagged divergence.
- Rewrite bites and stings around removal and observation: tick removal with tweezers at the skin (NHS, and CDC once fetched), scrape a bee sting sideways, cold compress, and the 999 and 911 triggers for airway swelling. Drop the FM's smothering advice.
- Add a medication basics section that the FM does not have: how to read the FDA Drug Facts label, dosing and interval pages from the NHS Medicines A to Z for paracetamol, ibuprofen, antihistamines and oral rehydration, and the WHO Essential Medicines list as the kit-building reference. Replace the FM herbal medicines section with NCCIH Herbs at a Glance evidence statements.
- Reorder environmental injury by what the reader can observe: the NHS and ATP 4-02.11 symptom lists separate heat exhaustion (pale, sweating) from heatstroke (hot, dry, confused), and mild from severe hypothermia; cooling and rewarming steps follow each identification.
- Keep the maritime guides as the depth layer for multi-day care with no evacuation: nursing an injured person, fever and infection, dental pain, and immersion hypothermia, which no first aid guideline covers.

## Tile 2: Shelter

### Current narrative

FM 21-76 chapter 5 has three sections: an introduction, shelter site selection, and types of shelters. The site section is a short list of conditions (near water and materials, away from flood, rockfall, insects, and dead trees) and a soldier-specific checklist about concealment and escape routes. The types section is a catalogue of builds on issued kit: poncho lean-to, poncho tent, three-pole and one-pole parachute tepees, field-expedient lean-to, swamp bed, natural shelters, debris hut, tree-pit snow shelter, beach shade, and desert shelters. It is written for an evader with a poncho, parachute, and cordage, so it never mentions a tent, a tarp with grommets, a sleeping pad, a sleeping bag rating, or a house. A civilian daily reader gets no guidance on pitching and anchoring a tent in wind, on how much ground insulation a body needs, on the snow trench, quinzhee, or igloo beyond four bullets on a tree pit, on carbon monoxide inside a shelter, or on sheltering at home during a storm, outage, or chemical release. Chapter 15 carries the cold-region shelters (snow cave, snow trench, molded dome) as prose under one plate, and its wind chill figure 15-1 is the pre-2001 table and must not ship.

## Tile 3: Fire

### Current narrative

FM 21-76 chapter 7 covers fire in six sections: basic fire principles (the fire triangle), site selection and preparation (the fire wall and the Dakota fire hole), fire material selection (tinder, kindling, fuel), how to build a fire (tepee, lean-to, cross-ditch, pyramid lays), how to light a fire (matches, convex lens, metal match, flint and steel, fire-plow, bow and drill), and helpful hints. Chapter 15 adds a short cold-region fire section. The chapter is written for a soldier evading capture in 1992, so it spends its ignition pages on friction methods and a "metal match", treats the fire hole as a way to hide light, and says nothing about permits, burn bans, fire danger ratings, or campground fire rings. A civilian daily reader gets no guidance on when a fire should not be lit, no stove content at all (canister, liquid fuel, alcohol, or wood-gas), no extinguishing procedure, no cooking-fire technique, and no carbon monoxide warning for stoves and heaters used inside a tent, vehicle, or shelter. The tinder, kindling, and fuel plate (figure 7-4) is the chapter's one tabular asset and is not on Commons.

## Tile 4: Water

### Current narrative

FM 21-76 chapter 6 (Water Procurement) covers water sources by climate, belowground and aboveground solar stills, purification by boiling, iodine, and chlorine, and an improvised layered filter. Chapter 17 (Crossing Water Obstacles) covers reading a river before fording, the pole and rope-team crossing methods, swimming rapids feet first, building brush and log rafts, and trouser flotation. Both chapters are written for a soldier who is evading and alone, so they assume no equipment, no bridge, and no option to wait, and the water-treatment numbers come from 1992 (five drops of 2 percent iodine per canteen, chlorine "tablets") with no mention of chlorine dioxide, ultraviolet devices, pore-size ratings, or Cryptosporidium. A civilian daily reader lacks current agency dosing for household bleach, the filter-versus-purifier distinction, a method-by-pathogen table, an emergency household water plan (how much to store, where water hides in a house), any treatment of drowning or cold-water immersion, and any advice to turn back from a crossing. The manual also spends pages on raft building that the reader will rarely use, while the two highest-frequency civilian situations, a boil-water notice and a knee-deep stream on a trail, get a few sentences.

## Tile 5: Food

### Current narrative
FM 21-76 Chapter 8 covers animals for food, traps and snares, killing devices, fishing devices, and the preparation of fish and game for cooking and storage. Chapter 9 covers the edibility of plants (the universal edibility test), temperate, tropical, and desert food plants, seaweeds, plants for medicine, and miscellaneous uses of plants. Both chapters address a soldier evading capture with no legal constraint, no thermometer, and no stored food, so they teach snares, deadfalls, gill nets, fish poison, and field smoking as normal practice, and they treat a 1992 edibility checklist as the answer to an unknown plant. A civilian reader who opens the tile daily lacks the material that a modern authority would put first: what to keep in the house and how long it stays safe, what to throw out after a power failure or flood, what internal temperature makes game safe, which wild-animal diseases hunters should know about, what the law permits when foraging, fishing, or trapping, and where to check whether a species is plausible in the reader's region. The manual's appendices B and C (111 edible and 17 poisonous species) are the only species data the tile carries today, and the manual carries no regional distribution, season, or nutrition values at all.

## Tile 6: Poisonous Plants

### Current narrative

FM 21-76 chapter 10 has six sections: Introduction, How Plants Poison, All About Plants, Rules For Avoiding Poisonous Plants, Contact Dermatitis, and Ingestion Poisoning, with 17 species accounts in Appendix C. The chapter frames poisoning as a hazard to a soldier who must eat unfamiliar plants to stay alive, so it spends its length on rules of thumb (milky sap, three leaves, umbrella flowers) and on the universal edibility test rather than on identification of named species. Its first aid is 1992 military practice: wash with soap and water for contact, and induce vomiting for ingestion, which current consumer guidance (NHS, MedlinePlus) reverses. It names no poison centre, no phone number, no plant that a child or a dog meets in a garden, no giant hogweed or wild parsnip, no mushroom syndromes, and no regional list. A civilian reader who uses the tile daily needs the opposite emphasis: which species in their region hurt people, how to tell a deadly plant from its edible lookalike, what to do in the first ten minutes, and whom to call.

### Narrative shift

- Lead with contact dermatitis, because the NPDS data and NIOSH text show it is the exposure most readers meet; the FDA consumer update becomes the tile's primary authority for poison ivy, oak and sumac, and NIOSH supplies the dose, surface persistence and burning warnings.

## Tile 7: Dangerous Animals

### Current narrative

FM 21-76 chapter 11 runs from insects and arachnids through leeches, bats, venomous snakes by region (Americas, Europe, Africa and Asia, Australasia), dangerous lizards, and river, estuary and saltwater hazards. It frames every animal as a threat to a soldier evading in unfamiliar terrain, so the emphasis falls on worldwide venomous species lists and on avoidance, with first aid delegated to the 1992 Army first-aid doctrine. The snake section still describes cut-and-suction kits and constricting bands, which the 2024 AHA and Red Cross first aid guidelines, the WMS pit viper guidelines and the CDC Yellow Book now reject. A civilian daily reader gets no tick removal method, no tick-borne or mosquito-borne disease guidance, no rabies exposure rule, nothing on bears, cougars, moose or dogs, and no regional species data they can look up by place. The chapter's species accounts (Appendix E in the FM, and the 1968 Navy "Poisonous Snakes of the World" already in the ledger) remain useful as public-domain illustrations and range notes, and the protocols around them need replacing.

## Tile 8: Tools and Cordage

### Current narrative

FM 21-76 chapter 12 has eight sections: Introduction, Clubs, Edged Weapons, Other Expedient Weapons, Lashing and Cordage, Rucksack Construction, Clothing and Insulation, and Cooking and Eating Utensils. Its figures 12-1 to 12-11 show clubs, stone and bone knives, a bow and arrow, a bola, plant-fibre cordage, packs, and bamboo containers, and the chapter frames every item as a weapon or field-expedient replacement for issued kit that a soldier has lost. The Lashing and Cordage section names the round turn and two half hitches, the prusik, the girth hitch, and the overhand knot but draws none of them; there is no bowline, no lashing figure, and no tying sequence anywhere in the chapter (this was confirmed in docs/research/process-node-sources.md). A civilian who reads for half an hour a day wants what the chapter omits: a small set of knots with step figures, square and shear lashing, how to whip and coil rope, how much a rope or a cord holds and how much a knot takes away, how to use and sharpen a knife, an axe, and a saw without injury, and how to fix a strap, a pole, or a torn fabric. The chapter's weapons content (clubs, bolas, spears, bows) belongs in an archive for a civilian reader, and its 1992 material list (parachute cord as an afterthought, no synthetic-rope properties) predates the modern cordage a reader actually carries.

## Tile 9: Direction Finding

### Current narrative
FM 21-76 chapter 18 teaches six field methods for finding north without a map: the shadow-tip stick, the watch method for each hemisphere, the moon, the Big Dipper and Cassiopeia pointing to Polaris, the Southern Cross, and an improvised magnetised needle floated on water or hung on thread. The chapter assumes an evader who has lost all equipment and needs a rough north-south line to walk away from a threat, so it never mentions a map, a bought compass, declination, or a GPS receiver, and it gives no numbers for the error of any method. The figures are 1992 line art and the section on improvised compasses has no figure at all. A civilian reader spends most navigation time with a topographic map, a baseplate compass, and a phone, and the questions that reader asks (what declination to set, how far to trust a phone fix under trees, what to do on realising they are lost) have no answer in the chapter. The sun and star methods remain useful as checks on a compass or phone, and the current US Army map-reading manuals cover them with the numbers FM 21-76 leaves out.

## Tile 10: Signaling and Rescue

### Current narrative
FM 21-76 chapter 19 covers the means of signaling (fire, smoke, the MK-3 mirror, flashlights, flares, panels, whistles, radios), the codes (SOS, ground-to-air panel symbols, body signals), and the procedure for vectoring a rescue aircraft by radio. It frames signaling as a soldier's problem: the reader is downed aircrew or an evader, the radio is a military survival set, and the text weighs the risk of being seen by the enemy against the chance of recovery. The 1992 edition predates 406 MHz personal locator beacons, satellite messengers, and the 2009 end of satellite monitoring on 121.5 MHz, so its electronic section is out of date. It says nothing about how a civilian search is organized, when a search starts, what searchers look for, what to tell a 911 dispatcher, or the consumer radio services (FRS, GMRS, marine VHF, amateur) a hiker or boater actually carries. The panel code and body-signal figures remain correct because the FAA and Transport Canada still print the same table.

## Tile 11: Environments

### Current narrative
FM 21-76 chapters 13 to 16 cover desert, tropical, cold weather, and sea survival in that order. Chapter 13 describes desert terrain and gives an hourly water rule and a daily water chart; chapter 14 describes jungle types, travel, and tropical food plants; chapter 15 gives cold regions, a wind chill table, cold injuries, shelters, fire, and weather signs; chapter 16 gives raft procedures, sea water, sharks, and reaching shore. The framing is a downed aircrew or isolated soldier in uniform with issued rafts and flares, and the medical content reflects 1992 Army practice. A civilian daily reader gets an obsolete wind chill table (figure 15-1 is the pre-2001 Siple-Passel index), no heat index or wet bulb globe temperature, no avalanche content, no lightning content, no cold-water immersion physiology beyond one survival-time table, no mosquito-borne disease guidance beyond insect warnings, and no way to read a forecast or a barometer. The four chapters also treat each environment as a place to be rescued from rather than a climate the reader lives in, so there is no link to where the reader is or what season it is.

## Tile 12: Man-Made Hazards

### Current narrative
FM 21-76 chapter 23 has four sections: Introduction, The Nuclear Environment, Biological Environments, and Chemical Environments. The nuclear section covers blast, thermal and radiation effects, fallout, shielding by mass and distance, expedient shelters, radiation sickness, decontamination, and finding protected water and food in a fallout zone. The biological and chemical sections describe agents, detection by sight and smell, protective masks and clothing, and decontamination as a soldier who carries issued equipment would do them. The framing is a 1992 US Army battlefield: the reader has a mask, a radiac meter, and detection kits, and the threat is an enemy strike. A civilian daily reader gets none of the household material: no alerts and warning channels, no family plan or supply kit, no pandemic or infection control, no power, water or communications outage, no wildfire smoke or carbon monoxide, and no current agency thresholds for sheltering, potassium iodide, food discard, or drinking water after a release.
